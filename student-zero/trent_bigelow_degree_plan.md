### PLA Entry #1 — Quantitative Reasoning & Data Literacy

**Competency:** Core Foundation 5.1
**Tier:** 3
**Evidence:**
1. USC transcript — MATH 117: Pre-Calculus (Grade: A, 2007)
2. Professional portfolio: documented examples of data analysis supporting product decisions at BILL and prior companies (available on request, redacted for confidentiality)

**Reflection (300 words):**
My quantitative foundation began formally at USC but has been reinforced and extended through 15 years of product work in data-driven environments. In product management, quantitative reasoning is not optional — every prioritization decision requires reasoning about expected value, statistical significance, and the difference between correlation and causation.

At BILL, I've owned metrics for AI product features where the stakes of quantitative error are high. Getting a confidence interval wrong in a product experiment costs real money and misleads the team. Over the years I've made — and caught — every common quantitative mistake: base rate neglect when evaluating new features, overconfidence in small samples, confusing median and mean in reporting. Each mistake taught me the underlying concept more durably than a course would have.

My work involves interpreting statistical reports from data science partners, designing experiment parameters, and communicating quantitative findings to non-technical audiences (executives, board members, customers). The competency standard asks that a learner "translate a data finding into a clear, non-technical recommendation" — I've done this hundreds of times in writing, in meetings, and in product reviews.

The USC transcript provides formal evidence of the mathematical foundation. The professional portfolio provides evidence of applied, current practice.

### PLA Entry #5 — Systems Thinking & Complexity

**Competency:** Core Foundation 5.5
**Tier:** 3
**Evidence:**
1. Professional portfolio: documented organizational design work, product architecture decisions, and cross-system analysis at multiple companies

**Reflection:**
Systems thinking became explicit to me when I realized that the most common product mistakes — metrics that move in the wrong direction when you optimize for what you're measuring, features that create engagement loops with unintended consequences, organizational changes that produce the opposite of the intended effect — are all systems thinking failures.

I've designed feedback mechanisms in products, mapped organizational interdependencies before major reorgs, and analyzed cascading effects of pricing and packaging decisions. This competency is one where professional experience genuinely exceeds what a course alone would develop. I can draw a causal loop diagram. More importantly, I've learned why ignoring feedback loops causes real damage.

The evidence is primarily professional and will be documented in portfolio artifacts (redacted as needed).
